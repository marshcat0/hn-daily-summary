12:Tb14,Back in 2015, when I was still at Google, there was a small group of code-golf enthusiasts spending hours trying to save just one byte on their solutions for weekly code-golf problems. No matter how hard you tried, there always seemed to be another Googler who found a shorter solution. And once a problem entered the challenge phase and every solution became public, it was always astonishing to read how brilliant other people&#x27;s solutions were.<p>11 years later, LLMs are evolving fast. A recent OpenAI post (&quot;Perplexity and Intelligence&quot;, <a href="https:&#x2F;&#x2F;openai.com&#x2F;index&#x2F;perplexity-and-intelligence&#x2F;" rel="nofollow">https:&#x2F;&#x2F;openai.com&#x2F;index&#x2F;perplexity-and-intelligence&#x2F;</a>) described researchers watching a model solve open math problems one after another in a Slack thread. I started to wonder: how would frontier models compete with the records that a small group of brilliant minds created with hours of effort each week?<p>So I built this benchmark. Frontier models do find a lot of new approaches (and very brilliant ones) to solve these problems. For example, in two problems (&quot;Surviving Queens&quot; and &quot;The Rolling Dice&quot;), frontier models use complex numbers to solve them in Python and Ruby (both languages have built-in complex number support). And for the &quot;(-5)-Smooth Numbers&quot; problem, where you have to write code to list the first n positive integers of the form (-2)^x⋅(-3)^y⋅(-5)^z, most solutions build them with nested loops or by growing a set, then sort. GPT-6.2 Astra gets them all from powers of 4, and earns a sole best with this brilliant solution:<p><pre><code>    g=lambda n:sorted(4**i%(2**999-5)%(2**49-3)for i in range(7000))[:n]
</code></pre>
