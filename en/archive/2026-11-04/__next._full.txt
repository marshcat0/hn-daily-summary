## 一、总体观察

今日热榜呈现出明显的“**信任与治理焦虑**”特征：前六条中有四条涉及 AI 厂商的**数据隐私、模型能力夸大、默认设置变更**等争议，讨论热度远超技术本身。与此同时，AI 基础设施的**资本与硬件连锁反应**（债务、磁带存储）成为第二大类话题。纯技术/研究类内容（生成式 AI 数学入门、布尔电路嵌入神经网络）热度相对靠后，但质量值得关注。

18:T12e7,Hey HN, we’re Wei-Wei and Jeff. We’re building Momentic, QA agents that autonomously plan, explore, and bug-bash controls and user flows in your web, iOS, Android, and (soon) desktop apps to catch bugs.<p>Here’s a demo: <a href="https:&#x2F;&#x2F;youtu.be&#x2F;BFsM-Se1Fh8" rel="nofollow">https:&#x2F;&#x2F;youtu.be&#x2F;BFsM-Se1Fh8</a><p>We’re both software engineers that spent our careers on dev tooling and infra across companies like Robinhood, Retool, WeWork, and Qualtrics. Testing was always a pain in the ass (esp Playwright and Selenium), so we built agents to do it for us.<p>Modern day software has a ton of bugs and the change failure rate is only growing faster as coding agent adoption scales. LLMs are really good at generating code, but reinforce their own assumptions, write a ton of useless tests, and are bad at visual understanding. On DeepSWE v1.1, Sonnet 5.5 hit 66.2% pass rate when banned from writing tests, versus 65.3% when allowed.<p>OSS end-to-end testing frameworks like Playwright, Selenium, or Maestro force you to hardcode implementation details like XPath, text, or aria-* attributes in your tests. Even with agents, these take a nontrivial amount of time and tokens to build and maintain.<p>In 2023, we saw Nat Friedman drive a browser with GPT-3 via natbot. We were inspired to use this technology to automate the most painful and tedious parts of software engineering: testing. We started with using LLMs to generate and maintain Playwright, but quickly realized that those primitives were fundamentally misaligned with the agentic SDLC. We think the future of QA is agents testing themselves, not writing scripts.<p>Mo is a custom agent harness for catching bugs that builds on the past 3 years of Momentic and millions of test traces from complex, real-world applications. Mo is built for recall: it finds the most real bugs, though Codex is a bit more precise at 86% vs 84%. The tradeoff is cost: Mo costs $2.67 per bug vs $0.80 for Codex, less than Claude Code and Devin.<p>- Self-organizing agent graph. Mo builds a directed acyclic graph (DAG) as it explores. Large product areas automatically fork into parallel sub-agents, each with their own browser or device for concurrent execution.<p>- Specialized agents for domain specific tasks. Planner agents reason over screenshots and video while dedicated locator agents inspect page structure to resolve plain-English interaction targets. Others include image analysis, video understanding, audio generation, and more.<p>- Parallel agents coordinate through cooperative resource leases. Agents can announce that they’re “using” or “modifying” overlapping resources or fixtures. Multiple “using” claims can coexist, but only a single “modifying” claim can be active on any particular one.<p>- Scaled infrastructure. Mo ships with a tunnel that lets users expose private sites and local apps to our hosted agents and infra. A single bug bash can spin up 100s of concurrent browsers and devices.<p>We benchmarked Mo against coding agents on 4 popular open-source apps: Gitea (58.8k stars), Metabase, Twenty (YC), and Saleor, with 1,396 human-labeled bugs.
